THE LAST SIGNAL FROM EUROPA

At 03:17 UTC, the listening station on Luna received a transmission from beneath the ice of Europa.

It lasted only eleven seconds.

No voice.
No coordinates.
No recognizable language.

Just a repeating sequence of prime numbers, followed by a map of the Jovian system drawn in pulses of radio light.

For six hours, nobody spoke in Mission Control.

The signal could not have come from Earth. The timing was wrong, the frequency was wrong, and the source had been triangulated to a point nearly twenty kilometers below Europa's frozen surface.

Then the second transmission arrived.

This one contained a single image.

A circle.

Inside it: four smaller circles, each moving around a central point.

Jupiter and its moons.

One of the moons was marked.

Europa.

Another pulse followed.

Then another.

The computers assembled them into a final message:

WE HEARD YOU.

Across Earth, observatories turned toward Jupiter. Satellites changed orientation. Deep-space antennas stopped their scheduled work and listened.

For the first time in human history, an entire planet waited for a reply from another world.

The response team argued for hours about what to send.

Mathematics?
Music?
Images of Earth?
A map?
A greeting?

In the end, they chose something simpler.

A sequence of prime numbers.

Then a map of the Solar System.

Then one final message encoded in the same mathematical structure the unknown sender had used:

WE HEAR YOU TOO.

The transmission left Earth at the speed of light.

No one in Mission Control celebrated.

They simply watched the countdown on the main display.

Forty-three minutes to Jupiter.

Forty-three minutes for a message to cross the darkness between two worlds.

And somewhere beneath kilometers of ancient alien ice, something might be waiting.

Listening.

Just as humanity had been listening for centuries.
